SECTION 01
How an agent works
What actually happens between typing a request and a file changing on disk.
Section 01
Harness and model
Harness — Cline
The program running inside your editor. It reads your files, decides what to send, applies the edits, and runs the commands.
It has no intelligence of its own. It is plumbing, and plumbing is where a surprising number of failures live.
Model — Gemini, Mistral
A service somewhere else that receives text and predicts what text should come next. It never sees your disk.
You can swap it for another in three clicks. You will do exactly that when a free allowance runs out.

Section 01
The loop the agent runs
—Read. The harness opens the files it thinks are relevant and puts their contents into the message.
—Plan and edit. The model replies with a change to make. The harness writes it to disk.
—Run and observe. The harness runs your tests, reads the output, and sends the result back.
—Then it goes round again. One sentence from you becomes four to ten trips through this loop.

SECTION 02
What it costs, and why it fails
Three limits you will meet before lunch, and the reason none of them are bugs.
Section 02
Context and its limits
—The model has no memory. Between one request and the next it retains nothing at all.
—So everything is re-sent. Every request carries the whole conversation and every file read so far.
—The context window is the ceiling on how much can be re-sent at once. Past it, the earliest parts fall away.
—A long conversation therefore makes an agent worse, not better. Start a new task for each feature.

Your free allowance, in one sum
one instruction from you -> 4 to 10 requests a 2-hour lab ~ 20 instructions ~ 100 to 200 requests free tier, Gemini 2.5 Flash 250 requests per day 10 requests per minute
One lab session is roughly one day of your free allowance. There is no spare.
Ten per minute also means the agent pauses about six seconds between steps. That is the limit working, not a fault.
Everything that cuts your request count is also just good engineering.

Section 02
Why an agent invents things
—It predicts, it does not look up. The reply is the text most likely to follow your request, not a fact retrieved from anywhere.
—A plausible function is indistinguishable from a real one. A library call that ought to exist looks exactly like one that does.
—It cannot warn you. Not knowing and knowing feel identical from the inside, so both come out equally confident.
—Which leaves one rule: you check by running something, never by reading something that sounds right.

SECTION 04
The Four Gates
The same request, routed through four points where a person decides.
Section 04
Two routes to the same app
Asking directly
Faster to something on screen, and right often enough that it feels like it works.
You cannot say what it should do, so you cannot say whether it does it. Nothing is checkable.
Through the gates
Slower to the first screen, and you decide four times along the way instead of once at the end.
Fewer requests too, because the agent stops guessing what you meant and backtracking.

Section 04
Four gates, four decisions
—Intent. You write one paragraph: who it is for, what problem it solves, what finished looks like.
—Spec. The agent drafts requirements. Each one needs a criterion you can check by running something.
—Plan. The agent proposes the files and a task list. One task owns one file, and no file has two owners.
—Build. One task at a time. Tests run, you read the diff, and only then does the next task start.

Section 04
You have seen these gates before
The gate you run
Intent, then spec, then plan, then build one task at a time, then ship.
Five steps that fit in a two-hour lab and produce four short documents.
What it is called elsewhere
Requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment — the software lifecycle, taught since the 1970s.
The agent needs these written down for the same reason a team of people does. Neither can read your mind.

The words used in industry
—Intent. The business goal before anyone has decided how to build it. Your Gate 1.
—Units. The independent pieces an intent breaks into. Next week each of you owns one.
—Bolts. Build cycles measured in hours rather than the two to six weeks of a sprint.

Read the reference before you leave
—Everyone does this, whether your own version works or not. The prompts are in labs/EXPLAIN.md.
—Ask it to break the code. Change the 2/n to 1/n, run the tests, watch which two fail, then change it back.

—Reading code you did not write, with an AI explaining it, is the most common way these tools are used at work.
